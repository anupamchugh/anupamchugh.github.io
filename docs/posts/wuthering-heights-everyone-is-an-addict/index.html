Wuthering Heights: We Inherit Damage

We inherit damage from the people closest to us.

Mr. Earnshaw brings home a boy and names him Heathcliff — after a son he already buried. That's where the whole story starts. Not with a love story, not with the moors. With a father's grief and a name he shouldn't have reused.

Catherine doesn't find Heathcliff. Her father puts him in her life. He's under her bed one night and that's it — she claims him the way children claim things, totally and without negotiation. They grow up feral together on the moors, and by the time anyone notices what's happening between them it's already too late to undo. Hindley resents the boy his father loved more than him, but that's the slower poison — it does its work in the background.

Then Catherine marries Edgar Linton. Edgar is gentle. Edgar is wealthy. Edgar is the kind of man you marry when you're trying to correct something. He's the life Catherine chooses, and there's nothing wrong with him except that he isn't the boy her father brought home. Heathcliff is the life her father chose for her before she could choose for herself, and you can't undo that. You can marry around it. You can build a whole house around it. But it's still there under the bed where she first found him.

She tries to hold both — the husband and the lover, the safe life and the wild one. Catherine doesn't choose wrong. She just can't stop choosing. She wants the moors and the manor, the comfort and the chaos, and when neither man will let her keep the arrangement she breaks. She spirals. She miscarries. She dies. Heathcliff races to see her and arrives too late. All he can do is beg her ghost to haunt him, which is the most Heathcliff thing possible — even his grief is a demand.

Hindley drinks himself into ruin. The house falls apart. Everyone downstream of Mr. Earnshaw's one act of love — bringing a boy home, giving him a dead son's name — ends up destroyed by it. The father meant well. That's the worst part.

Fennell's version doesn't treat this like a romance. It treats it like a family that never recovered from one man's grief. The love story everyone talks about is just the loudest symptom. Underneath it, a father brought something into his house that his house couldn't survive.

Everyone inherited something from him — the grief, the name, the house, the need to be loved first. By the end, nobody had escaped it.

I finished the film exhausted.
