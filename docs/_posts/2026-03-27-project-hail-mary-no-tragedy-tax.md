There's no arrival here — no slow decoding of an alien language that cracks open your perception of time. Nothing interstellar about the gap between a father and the daughter aging without him. Nobody's the martian, alone on a planet, talking to a camera because there's no one else to talk to. And there's no gravity holding anyone down with a grief they can't escape. Just a hail mary. And somehow it works.

Project Hail Mary sidesteps all of it. Even the flashbacks to Earth aren't about someone Grace misses — they're about how he ended up on the ship in the first place. Grace is alone for a while before he meets Rocky — an alien engineer whose sun is dying too — and after that it's two species who can't breathe the same air figuring out how to save each other. The science is there but it's not the point. They breeze past learning each other's language because the point isn't the language, it's what they do with it.

Every one of those films hands the hero a kid they're going to lose. Arrival — a daughter who hasn't been born yet but you already know she dies. Interstellar — one aging decades away on a screen you can't touch. Gravity — gone before the movie even starts. That's how sci-fi makes you care. Lost kids. Kids you can't reach. Hail Mary runs on Astrophage instead — the organism eating the sun, repurposed as the fuel that powers the ship. And instead of a child to mourn, there's Rocky, who finds Grace before Grace finds him.

Grace gives up going home to save Rocky. In another film that's the tragedy. Here he just chooses. The last scene is Grace on Erid, Rocky's planet, teaching alien kids. He's not stranded. He picked this life. And it looks like a good one.

I walked out happy for Grace. Not relieved, not drained — happy. Sci-fi almost never lets you have that.
