# Every Wave Is Not Your Wave

Varkala, Kerala. End of the surfing season. I've been coming here every year since 2022 — one week of classes, same beach, same routine.

The approach is the same. I'm on the board, waves are coming, and every one looks like the one. I start paddling. My instructor — either behind or in front with hands on the board — holds me back. Sometimes.

Wave after wave passes. Three. Four. Then: "PADDLE PADDLE PADDLE — POP UP!"

I ride it. More often than I would have alone. Not because I picked the right wave. Or got the technique fully right. But because someone who's watched ten thousand waves break picked it for me.

The wave doesn't teach you how to surf. The waiting does. Every wave an instructor holds you back from — that was the lesson. Feeling the pull before the break.

I keep coming back because I still can't read the water on my own. Not for the thrill. Not to get better at some measurable thing. I surf for the love of surfing.

Turns out that's the same skill I kinda used.

I managed Better Programming on Medium for two years. A programming publication. Thousands of submissions from people from all walks of life. My job wasn't picking the best ones. My job was holding boards back.

A piece that skimmed the surface of what it tried to explain — held back. A draft on a topic already covered — held back. Not rejected — although it always feels like it. Held. Because paddling for the wrong wave, or with ten others on the same one, rarely helps anyone.

The learning happens in the drafts a writer writes and an editor reads — or skims through — to realize where you don't actually understand the thing you set out to explain. That's it. That's the whole function. Writing for the love of writing. Publishing for the love of what writing does to the person doing it.

Some writers came back with something better. Some left for publications that would push them into everything. I understood. Every article I didn't publish got picked up somewhere else. Those pieces drove numbers. Bigger waves, everyone riding them, no one asking where they lead.

Most publishing platforms work like most surf schools — take every surfer, push them into every wave, let them paddle everything. Don't read the sea. Don't hold the board back. More waves, more pay.

Platforms curate for the platform. Publications curate for the writer. And if a surfer doesn't get what they need from a school that holds them back, they move to one that doesn't. You know why — online writing sucks you into a never-ending loop.

Better Programming was the other kind. The kind that held boards back.

Better Programming is kaput. The platforms are fine.

Perhaps that's how it works. The school that teaches you to wait doesn't survive in a market that rewards volume. The economics don't support restraint. AI didn't create this problem. AI just made the paddle count infinite.

And the real loss isn't the publication. It's the function. Nobody in the lineup is paid to hold the board back anymore. Writers still need someone who's watched ten thousand submissions to say — not this one. But that role, the one that lives between rejection and instruction, has no viable business model. Never did, apparently.

My instructors in Varkala watch the ocean for the love of it. I watched submissions. Neither of us got paid to say yes. We got paid — for the nos.

Paddle back in sometimes.

I'll be back next year. Same beach. I'll still need an instructor. I'll still be curious about how the new ones read the water.
